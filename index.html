Custom Quadcopter: Frame Design, FEA & Flight Electronics

Fusion 360FEA3D printingTeensy 4.0dRehmFlight Individual project · July 2026 – present

Designed my own compact quadcopter frame, proved in simulation that the arms could take flight loads with a safety factor of at least 2.5, then printed it, wired the flight electronics by hand, and flew it.

What I designed

  • Drew every frame part myself in Fusion 360 and joined them into one assembly.
  • Sized the center stack around the flight controller, ESC and a 4S battery.
  • Kept arms as short as the propellers allowed so the airframe stayed small and light.
  • Revised the frame twice, using simulation results to decide where material could come out.

Load case

InputValue
Thrust10 N up at each motor mount (40 N total)
Weight9.81 N down at the center body (≈ 1 kg)
MaterialABS (simulated)

What the analysis told me: the frame cleared its target with margin, so the electronics, not the structure, were the limiting part of the design. The study treats the frame as solid material, while a print is weaker along its layer lines, and I simulated ABS but printed in PLA to stay on budget. A fairer check would re-run the study with PLA properties and an impact load.

Flight electronics

ComponentRole
4S LiPo (14.8 V)Single power source, sized for all four motors at full throttle.
45 A ESCsConvert battery DC to three-phase drive and set each motor's speed.
Brushless motors ×4Produce thrust; speed differences tilt and turn the aircraft.
GY-521 IMUMeasures rotation rate and acceleration for attitude estimation.
Teensy 4.0Runs dRehmFlight: reads the IMU and corrects each motor's throttle in a closed loop.

Firmware setup (dRehmFlight)

  • IMU setup: Teensy reads GY-521 gyro and accelerometer data to estimate attitude.
  • Motor mixing: Quad-X mixing, each motor on its own output channel.
  • Spin direction: alternating rotation so opposing pairs cancel propeller torque.
  • Pilot input: throttle, roll, pitch and yaw mapped to attitude commands.

Testing & result

  • Confirmed ESC startup tones before arming, then checked each motor's response and spin direction on the bench.
  • Ran incremental ground tests, then flew it. V1 got off the ground and flew.
  • A later crash damaged the separate ESCs. V2 is being rebuilt around a single 4-in-1 ESC: it suits a frame this small, costs less than replacing four units, and cuts the number of hand-soldered joints. Goal: fully controllable flight.

04

Three-Car Train & Track Assembly

SolidWorksTolerancingMotion study Engineering Graphics, Texas Tech · Spring 2026

A train that works as a real mechanism: every part modeled from scratch, three cars coupled into one assembly on a track, with wheels that turn and drive the train forward.

What I designed

  • Modeled every component as its own part (bodies, chassis plates, bogies, couplers, axles, wheels), then built car sub-assemblies.
  • Designed detail features on each body, including the locomotive's boiler, stack and running gear.
  • Built the track as its own part so the wheels had a real surface to run on.
  • Set ±0.01 mm tolerances on mating features so wheels turn and couplers connect without binding.

Assembly structure

  • Locomotive: body · chassis · drive wheelsets · coupler
  • Passenger car: body · chassis · bogies · wheelsets · couplers
  • Coal car: body · chassis · bogies · wheelsets · coupler

Result

The motion study ran without interference: wheels turned on their axles, couplers held, and the train tracked along the rails, confirming the parts and tolerances worked together.

What I would change

±0.01 mm is tighter than a printed or machined part would hold. For a physical build, I'd open the wheel-to-axle clearance to something a real process can hit.
